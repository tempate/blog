## Reality

A _state-of-things_ is a combination of objects. I do not know what an object is, so just think of objects as the "essence" of the world, whatever that might mean.

An object has _internal_ and _external_ properties. The external properties of an object can change depending on the state-of-things the object is in. Whether an object is red or not, for example, is an external property, since it depends on the relation between the object and the other objects in the state-of-things. 

The internal properties of an object are its possibilities for combining with other objects. An object cannot exist in a vacuum. It can only exist in relation to other objects. Each object then contains the possibility of every state-of-things it can occur in. As an example, suppose that a human and a chair were objects. It is intrinsic to the definition of a chair that a human can sit on it, and it is intrinsic to the definition of a human that it can sit on a chair. The relation between a human and a chair is then inside the human and the chair. 

The structure of a state-of-things is the relation between its objects. I like to think of a state-of-things as a graph in which vertices are objects and edges are structure. In the example above, the chair and the human would be the vertices of the graph, and the edge would be their sitting relation.

_Reality_ says what states-of-things take place. We call the states-of-things that take place the _world_. Every state-of-things may or may not take place, so the world determines reality, because if we know all the states-of-things that take place, we also know all the states-of-things that do not.

I like to think of reality as a function $R$ that maps states-of-things to the set {"takes place", "does not take place"}. The _world_ is then the collection of all states-of-things $X$ such that $R(X) =$ "takes place". A state-of-things $X$ in which I have a cat is not part of the world, since I do not have a cat, so $R(X) =$ "does not take place", while a state-of-things $Y$ in which I have a dog is part of the world, since I do have a dog, so $R(Y) =$ "takes place".

## Language

A _picture_ is a representation, also called a model. A _proposition_ is a picture of a state-of-things. There can be a proposition of a state-of-things that does not take place, so propositions picture reality, not just the world. The sentence "the good is identical to the beautiful" is not a proposition, because it does not picture a state-of-things, while the sentence "the dog is sleeping on the couch" is a proposition, because it pictures a state-of-things in which a dog is sleeping on a couch.

_Language_ is the collection of all propositions. A _sign_ is the part of language we perceive, like a written or spoken word. A symbol is what different signs share when they mean the same thing. We call the symbol of an object its _name_.

In natural languages, many signs tend to share a symbol, and many symbols tend to share a sign. The written "screen", the spoken "screen", and the Spanish "pantalla" are three different signs for the same symbol. The sentences "2+2 is 4" and "there is one orange" use the sign "is" for two different symbols, equality and existence. In a perfect language, each symbol would only have one sign, and each sign would only have one symbol.

A _thought_ is a proposition that says something about the world, true or false. The proposition "it is raining or it is not raining" is not a thought, since it does not say anything about the world. It is always true. But the proposition "pigs can fly" is a thought, because it does say something about the world, and it can be either true or false. 

_Natural science_ is the collection of true thoughts. The thought "pigs can fly" is not part of natural science, because it is not true, while the thought "birds can fly" is part of natural science, because it is true. 

_Philosophy_ is the clarification of thoughts. It is not a body of teachings, or a natural science, but an activity. The result of philosophy is then not a collection of "philosophical propositions", but just how a collection of propositions becomes clearer.


## The relation between language and reality

Suppose I photograph a building. I can recognize the building when I look at the photograph, so there must be something in common between the photograph and the building. They share a _spatial form_, since the objects in the photograph are arranged like the objects in the building.

As above, a proposition pictures reality, so there must be something in common between the proposition and reality. The proposition "the dog is sleeping on the couch", however, is not arranged like a dog sleeping on a couch. What they have in common is more abstract. The dog and the couch stand in a certain relation in reality, and so do the words "dog" and "couch" stand in a certain relation in the proposition. We call whatever is in common between a picture and reality its _logical form_.

A proposition can never explicitly _say_ its logical form. It can only _show_ it. The logical form is not a state-of-things that may or may not take place, so a proposition cannot picture it. Language can then say how things stand in the world, but it cannot say what it shares with the world in order to say it.
