---
title: Tractatus (Propositions 1 to 2.06)
date: 2026-10-15
---

What is the world? The world is the collection of all facts. Statements are pictures of facts, and every statement is either true or false. So the true statements describe the world completely, and knowing all of them also tells us all the false ones.

Statements can be simple or complex, and a complex statement is just a collection of simple ones. A simple statement is made of names, and each name refers to an object. Objects are not statements themselves. They belong to the world, not to language, and they combine into states-of-things.

Think of a state-of-things as a graph. Its vertices are objects, and its edges are its structure. Each vertex carries in its definition how it relates to the other vertices, so you can never define a vertex without also defining its edges. An object then does not exist in a vaccum, and contains the possibility of every state-of-things it can occur in.

The world is then the collection of all the states-of-things that take place. Just as knowing all true statements determines all the false ones, knowing all the states-of-things that take place also determines all the states-of-things that do not. 

Reality is the collection of all states-of-things, whether they take place or not. These states-of-things are independent of each other, so knowing that one takes place tells us nothing about whether another does.

What, then, do the world we see and the world described by true statements have in common? The objects. They give the world its substance. Without them, whether a statement had sense would depend on whether some other statement had sense, and the world would be nothing more than a collection of unverifiable statements.

The states-of-things can change, but the objects are constant. Their substance determines only their form, not their material properties, which arise only once objects combine into states-of-things. So the objects themselves are, for example, colorless, and we can distinguish them only by their essence.

This brings us back to the opening question. The world is not made of things but of how fixed, colorless objects happen to be arranged.
