## Language, thoughts, and science

_Language_ is the collection of all propositions. We call the propositions that make sense _thoughts_, and the thoughts that are true _natural science_.

The proposition "the good is identical to the beautiful" is not a thought, because it does not picture a state-of-things, so it is nonsense. The proposition "pigs can fly" is a thought, because it makes sense, but is not natural science, because it is not true. The proposition "birds can fly" is natural science, since it is true. 

## Signs and symbols

A _sign_ is the part of language we perceive, like a written or spoken word. A symbol is what different signs share when they mean the same thing. 

In natural languages, many signs tend to share a symbol, and many symbols tend to share a sign. The written "screen", the spoken "screen", and the Spanish "pantalla" are three different signs for the same symbol. The sign "is" in the sentence "2+2 is 4" represents the symbol for equality, while in the sentence "there is a house with a garden" represents the symbol for existence. In a perfect language, each symbol would have exactly one sign, and each sign exactly one symbol.

We call the symbols for simple objects _names_.

## Reality and the world

_Reality_ is a function that maps state-of-things to the set {takes place, does not take place}. We call the states-of-things that take place in reality the _world_.

A state-of-things $X$ in which I have a cat is a valid state-of-things, but it is not part of the world, since I do not have a cat, so reality maps $X$ to "does not take place". A state-of-things $Y$ in which I have a dog is part of the world, since reality maps $Y$ to "takes place".

## Propositions as pictures

A picture is a representation, also called a model. A proposition is a picture of a state-of-things that may or may not take place, so propositions picture reality, not just the world.

The proposition "the dog is sleeping on the couch" pictures a state-of-things in which the dog is sleeping on the couch. The dog may or may not be sleeping on the couch, so the proposition can be true or false, but it pictures the same state-of-things regardless.

## Showing and saying

Suppose I photograph my room. I can recognize my room when I look at the photograph, so there must be something in common between the photograph and the room. They share a _spatial form_, since the objects in the photograph are arranged like the objects in the room.

A proposition pictures reality, so there must be something in common between the proposition and reality. The proposition "the dog is sleeping on the couch", however, is not arranged like a dog sleeping on a couch. What they have in common is more abstract. The dog and the couch stand in a certain relation in reality, and so do the words "dog" and "couch". We call whatever is in common between a picture and reality its _logical form_.

A proposition can never explicitly _say_ its logical form. It can only _show_ it. The logical form is not a state-of-things that may or may not take place, so a proposition cannot picture it. Language can then say how things stand in the world, but it cannot say what it shares with the world in order to say it.
